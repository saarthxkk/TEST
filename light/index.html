Activity 1 · Rectilinear Propagation

Activity 1: Light Travels in a Straight Line

Aim: To observe that light travels along a straight line path (rectilinear propagation) using a rubber tube and candle.

Materials Required: 🕯️ Lighted Candle 〰️ Flexible Rubber Tube 🪵 Table Stand 👁️ Observer's Eye
Procedure:
  1. 1. Place the lighted candle on the table and align the rubber tube in a straight horizontal line.
  2. 2. Look through the tube at the candle flame with one eye. Observe if light passes through.
  3. 3. Drag the middle of the tube or click "Coil Tube" to bend it. Observe whether the flame is still visible.
INTERACTIVE OPTICAL STAGE Drag the tube handle up or down to bend it!
👁️ Live Observation
The tube is perfectly straight. The light coming from the candle flame travels directly through the tube and is clearly visible to the observer's eye.
🏆 SCIENTIFIC CONCLUSION

Light travels along a straight line path. This fundamental property is called the rectilinear propagation of light. When the tube is bent or coiled, the light rays cannot turn or curve around the bend, so the flame is completely blocked.

📝 Check Yourself (Activity 1)
Q1. What is the property of light traveling in a straight line called?
Correct! "Rectilinear" comes from "recti" (straight) and "linear" (line). Light never bends on its own around an obstacle.
Q2. Why could the student NOT see the flame through the coiled rubber tube?
Correct! Because light cannot curve around obstacles, the rays strike the inner opaque wall of the bend and are absorbed.
Activity 2 · Plane Mirror Reflection

Activity 2: Image Formed by a Plane Mirror

Aim: To investigate the characteristics of the image formed by a plane mirror: object vs. image distance, virtual nature on a screen, and lateral inversion.

Materials Required: 🪞 Vertical Plane Mirror 🕯️ Lighted Candle (Object) 📏 Centimeter Ruler 📄 Cardboard Screen
Procedure:
  1. 1. Place the candle in front of the mirror. Observe the image formed behind the mirror.
  2. 2. Drag the candle closer and further away. Compare object distance (u) with image distance (v).
  3. 3. Drag the paper screen behind the mirror to test if the image can be caught on a screen.
OPTICAL BENCH · PLANE MIRROR Drag the candle horizontally to change distance!
Object Distance (u) 30.0 cm
Image Distance (v) 30.0 cm
Object Height (hₒ) 8.0 cm
Image Height (hᵢ) 8.0 cm
👁️ Live Observation
Image is formed behind the mirror at exactly the same distance as the candle in front (u = v = 30 cm). The image is erect, of identical size, and cannot be captured on the cardboard screen placed behind the mirror.
🏆 SCIENTIFIC CONCLUSION

The image formed by a plane mirror is always erect (upright), virtual (cannot be obtained on a screen), of the same size as the object, and formed at the same distance behind the mirror as the object is in front of it. In addition, the image is laterally inverted (left becomes right and right becomes left).

📝 Check Yourself (Activity 2)
Q1. An object is placed 35 cm in front of a plane mirror. What is the distance between the object and its image?
Correct! The image is 35 cm behind the mirror. Total distance from object to image = 35 cm + 35 cm = 70 cm.
Q2. Why is the word AMBULANCE painted as 'ƎƆИAJUᗺMA' on the front of emergency vehicles?
Correct! A plane mirror laterally inverts left and right, so the pre-inverted lettering is seen right-side-up and easily readable by preceding drivers.
Activity 3 · Concave Mirror Focus & Solar Burning

Activity 3: Real Image of the Sun by a Concave Mirror

Aim: To obtain a real image of the distant Sun on a sheet of paper using a concave mirror and observe solar energy concentration at the focus.

Materials Required: ☀️ Parallel Sunbeams 🔘 Concave Mirror on Stand 📄 Sheet of Paper (Screen) 📏 Ruler (Focal Scale)
Procedure:
  1. 1. Point the concave mirror towards the distant Sun so parallel sunlight rays hit its reflective curved surface.
  2. 2. Move the paper sheet back and forth along the axis until you get the smallest, sharpest, and brightest spot.
  3. 3. Hold the paper steadily at this point (the principal focus) for 3-5 seconds and watch for smoke!
SUNLIGHT CONVERGENCE · PRINCIPAL FOCUS Drag the paper to Focus F (20 cm) and hold steady!
Paper Distance from Mirror 32.0 cm
Focal Length (f) 20.0 cm
Spot Brightness & Heat 35% (Warm)
Hold Time at Focus 0.0 s
👁️ Live Observation
The paper is at 32 cm. The reflected sunrays form a wide, blurry circle of light. The heat is dispersed and the paper remains safe.
🏆 SCIENTIFIC CONCLUSION

The sharp, bright spot of light is the real image of the Sun formed at the principal focus (F) of the concave mirror. Because it can be received on a paper screen, it is a REAL image. The concentrated solar heat gathered from the entire mirror surface into a tiny pinpoint causes the paper to smoke and catch fire.

📝 Check Yourself (Activity 3)
Q1. Why does the paper start burning only when placed at the focus of the concave mirror?
Correct! A concave mirror is a converging mirror. It focuses sunlight from its entire aperture onto the tiny focal spot.
Q2. An image that can be formed and captured on a physical screen is called a:
Correct! By definition in physics, an image formed by actual intersecting rays that can be caught on a screen is a Real Image.

Activity 4: Image Formed by a Concave Mirror

Aim: To observe the nature, position, and relative size of the image formed by a concave mirror for different positions of the object, and verify with a screen and observation table.

🏆 SCIENTIFIC CONCLUSION

The image formed by a concave mirror can be smaller or larger, real or virtual, and erect or inverted, depending strictly on the distance of the object from the mirror! When the object is far away (beyond F), the image is real and inverted. When the object is placed very close (inside focus F), the image becomes virtual, erect, and magnified.

💡 Important Practical Uses of Concave Mirrors
🦷 Dentist's Mirror: When held close to a tooth (inside F), it produces an erect and magnified image of teeth.
🔦 Torches & Headlights: The bulb is placed at focus F, producing a powerful, parallel beam of light.
🪒 Shaving / Makeup Mirror: Provides an enlarged upright view of the face when held close.
☀️ Solar Furnaces: Huge concave mirrors concentrate sunlight at the focus to produce intense temperatures (>1000°C).
📝 Check Yourself (Activity 4)
Q1. If an object is placed at the center of curvature (C = 30 cm) of a concave mirror (f = 15 cm), where is the image formed?
Correct! For an object at C, 1/v = 1/f - 1/u = 1/15 - 1/30 = 1/30, so v = 30 cm (at C), m = -1 (equal size).
Q2. When a dentist holds a concave mirror very close to a tooth (inside focal length F), the image is:
Correct! When u < f, the reflected rays diverge and appear to meet behind the mirror, creating a virtual, upright, and magnified image.

Q2. Can a convex mirror ever form a real, inverted image of an object?
Correct! The rays always diverge after reflection from a convex mirror, so their extensions meet virtually behind the mirror.

A convex lens is thicker in the middle than at the edges and acts as a converging lens. When the object is placed outside the focus, it forms a real and inverted image that can be caught on a screen. When placed close to the lens (inside F), it forms an erect, virtual, and magnified image — which is why it is used as a magnifying glass!

📝 Check Yourself (Convex Lens)
Q1. Why is a convex lens called a 'converging lens'?
Correct! The shape bends parallel light rays inward towards the principal axis, converging them at the focus.
Q2. To use a convex lens as a magnifying glass to read tiny print, where must the print be placed?
Correct! When u < f, the refracted rays diverge and their virtual back-extensions form an upright, magnified virtual image.

Procedure:
  1. 1. Mount the concave lens (thinner in the middle) on the optical bench.

At u = 30 cm, the concave lens diverges the rays. Their backward virtual extensions meet at v = 10 cm in front of the lens. The image is always virtual, upright (erect), and smaller. The screen placed anywhere catches nothing.

👓 Everyday Applications of Lenses

🚪 Door Peephole: Uses a concave lens to provide a wide, miniature upright view of visitors standing outside.
🔭 Telescopes & Binoculars: Combine multiple convex and concave lenses to magnify distant stars and landscapes.
🔬 Microscopes & Projectors: High-powered convex lenses magnify microscopic cells or project movies onto large screens.

Correct! Textbook question: Sunrays diverge after passing through a concave lens, scattering heat over a wide area so paper never heats up.
Q2. Which lens is thicker in the middle and thinner at the edges?
Correct! By definition, a convex lens feels thicker in the center than at its edges.

Activity 5: Light Consists of Different Colours (Dispersion)

Aim: To demonstrate that white sunlight is composed of seven colours by passing a narrow beam through a glass prism (dispersion) and observe how natural rainbows form.

Materials Required: ☀️ Narrow White Sunlight Beam 💎 Glass Triangular Prism 📄 White Paper Screen 🚪 Darkened Room with Slit
Procedure:
  1. 1. In a dark room, allow a narrow beam of white sunlight from a tiny window hole to fall on one face of a glass prism.
  2. 2. Turn the prism so the light exiting the other face falls onto a white cardboard screen.
  3. 3. Observe the band of seven colours (VIBGYOR). Note which colour bends the most and which bends the least!

Incident Light White Sunlight
Most Bent Colour Violet (λ ≈ 400 nm, n = 1.536)
Least Bent Colour Red (λ ≈ 700 nm, n = 1.512)

👁️ Live Observation
The white beam strikes the prism and splits inside into seven distinct colours. On the white screen, a beautiful band of VIBGYOR (Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, Red) is visible. Violet ray bends the most (maximum deviation) while Red ray bends the least.
🏆 SCIENTIFIC CONCLUSION

White sunlight is not a single colour, but a mixture of seven colours: Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, and Red (VIBGYOR). The phenomenon of splitting white light into its component colours when passing through a transparent medium is called DISPERSION. The resulting coloured band is called a SPECTRUM.

📝 Check Yourself (Activity 5)
Q1. When white light passes through a glass prism, which colour deviates (bends) the MOST?
Correct! Violet light has the shortest wavelength and highest refractive index in glass, so it slows down and bends the most.
Q2. Where must the Sun be located for an observer to see a natural rainbow in the sky?
Correct! A rainbow is produced when sunlight enters raindrops, undergoes internal reflection, and emerges towards the observer whose back is to the Sun.

Fascinating Fast Facts About Light

🚀
Speed of Light in Vacuum: Light travels at an incredible speed of approximately 300,000 kilometres per second (c ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s). Nothing in the known universe can travel faster!
☀️
Journey from the Sun: The Sun is nearly 150 million kilometres away from Earth. Even at the speed of light, sunlight takes about 8 minutes and 20 seconds to reach your eyes!
🪞
Rear-View Mirror Warning: Convex mirrors make objects look smaller and further away than they actually are. That is why they are engraved with: "Objects in the mirror are closer than they appear"!
🌈
Sir Isaac Newton's Discovery: In 1666, 23-year-old Isaac Newton used a glass prism in a dark room to prove that white sunlight is not pure white, but composed of the seven rainbow colours!

Optics Glossary (14 Key Terms)

Ray: A straight line with an arrow showing the direction in which light travels.
Rectilinear Propagation: The fundamental property of light traveling along straight-line paths.
Reflection: The bouncing back of light rays from the polished surface of an object into the same medium.
Real Image: An image formed by the actual intersection of reflected or refracted light rays that CAN be obtained on a physical screen.
Virtual Image: An image from which rays only appear to diverge and which CANNOT be obtained on a physical screen.
Lateral Inversion: The phenomenon in which the left side of an object appears as the right side in its mirror image, and vice-versa.
Concave Mirror: A spherical mirror whose reflecting surface is curved inwards like the inside of a spoon; acts as a converging mirror.
Convex Mirror: A spherical mirror whose reflecting surface is curved outwards like the back of a spoon; acts as a diverging mirror.
Principal Focus (F): The point on the principal axis where rays parallel to the axis converge or appear to diverge from after reflection/refraction.
Converging Lens: A lens (such as a convex lens) that is thicker in the middle and bends parallel rays inwards towards a common focal point.
Diverging Lens: A lens (such as a concave lens) that is thinner in the middle and spreads parallel rays outwards away from the axis.
Dispersion: The phenomenon of splitting of white light into its constituent seven colours upon passing through a prism.
Spectrum: The distinct coloured band of seven colours (VIBGYOR) obtained when white light undergoes dispersion.
Persistence of Vision: The ability of the human eye to retain an image for about 1/16th of a second, causing rapid color sequences to blend smoothly.
